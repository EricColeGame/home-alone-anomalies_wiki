The premise of [Home Alone: Anomalies](https://www.roblox.com/games/87468080405188/Home-Alone-Anomalies) is simple to state and stressful to play: you are home alone, visitors keep arriving at the door, and not everyone is who they claim to be. The official Roblox page describes a loop built on checking cameras, listening to what visitors say, opening the door when someone genuinely needs help, and turning off the porch light when something feels off. Survive the night and you earn cash.

That is the version the developers advertise. Everything beyond it — which visitors appear, what each anomaly looks like, how many endings exist — is either documented in passing by third-party guides or not documented at all. This article separates those layers so you know what you are actually walking into.

## What the official Roblox page confirms

The place record retrieved on October 14, 2026 lists 3 AM Productions and Freeground as the creators. The experience was created on May 4, 2026 and last updated on October 10, 2026. Servers hold up to 18 players.

The advertised feature list from the official description is short and specific:

- Checking cameras
- Listening to what visitors say
- Opening the door if a visitor needs help
- Turning off the porch light if something is off
- Surviving the night to earn cash
- Troll items for friends

By October 14, 2026, the experience had accumulated roughly 208 million visits. That number reflects popularity, not difficulty — it tells you the loop is easy to pick up, not that it is easy to master.

The same page documents UPDATE 12, dated October 10, 2026, which added a Halloween event with collectible pumpkins and a Halloween Pass, costumed visitors, new pets (Black Cat, White Cat, Crawler Cat), a Top 100 pumpkin leaderboard with a Celestial Cat reward, and assorted bug fixes. If you are reading this after October 2026, check the official page directly, because seasonal content rotates and the update number may have moved on.

## The core decision: trust or refuse

The whole experience funnels into one recurring choice. A visitor is at the door. You have cameras, you have a porch light, and you have your own ears. The official wording is unusually careful: open the door *if they need help*, turn off the porch light *if something is off*. Both verbs are conditional. Neither is a guarantee.

That framing matters because it implies the game is testing judgment, not reflexes. Camera checks and dialogue lines are inputs. The door and the light are outputs. If you open for everyone, you will eventually open for the wrong thing. If you refuse everyone, you will presumably fail the visitors who actually needed you. The official page does not state what happens when you get it wrong, and it does not describe a fail state in detail.

## What third-party guides claim — and why you should treat it as secondary

Community write-ups describe visitor behaviours, audio cues, and specific anomaly types in more detail than the official page does. These are third-party observations, not developer documentation. They can be useful for pattern recognition, but they are also the kind of content that goes stale the moment an update ships — and UPDATE 12 shipped with "costumed visitors," which by its nature reshuffles what a normal visitor looks like on any given night.

| Information type | Source | Reliability |
|---|---|---|
| Core loop, controls described in prose, update history | Official Roblox page | Authoritative |
| Visitor behaviour patterns, cue lists, anomaly descriptions | Third-party guides | Useful but secondary; verify in-game |
| Exact anomaly names, full visitor roster, house layout, endings, code strings | Not documented in either | Do not trust anyone who states these confidently |

If a guide online hands you a complete numbered list of anomalies or a set of promotional codes, treat that as unverified. The official page documents no promo codes. Do not go looking for them, and do not believe a video that claims to have them.

## Why 18 players changes the experience

Most "stay home and survive" horror loops are solo affairs. This one allows up to 18 players per server, which the official page confirms. A full server means more doors being answered, more porch lights being flipped, and more chances for one player's mistake to ripple outward. It also means the experience is social by design, which lines up with the advertised "troll items for friends" — the developer intent clearly includes messing with people you know, not just outlasting a scripted threat.

Whether that cooperation is structured (shared survival, shared payout) or incidental is not something the official description spells out. Check it yourself before assuming.

## What is simply not documented

Be suspicious of anyone who claims to know the following from official sources:

- A complete visitor list or anomaly taxonomy
- Exact cash amounts or payout formulas per night
- A specific number of nights, endings, or win conditions
- Keyboard hotkeys or control bindings
- Drop rates for pets or Halloween items
- Any promotional code

None of that appears in the verified official description. The fastest way to confirm any of it is to open the experience page, read the current description, and then play a round and observe directly. Patch notes live on the page and on the creator groups, not in aggregator articles.

## A practical way to approach a night

Start by treating the camera as your baseline and the dialogue as your tiebreaker. Establish what a normal visitor sounds like before you start judging outliers — UPDATE 12's costumed visitors mean "normal" itself shifts during the Halloween event, so your reference point matters. Use the porch light as your low-risk refusal, since the official page frames it as the response to something being off, and reserve opening the door for cases where the visitor's need for help is clear.

None of this guarantees survival. It is a reasoning framework built from what the developers actually wrote, not a script.
