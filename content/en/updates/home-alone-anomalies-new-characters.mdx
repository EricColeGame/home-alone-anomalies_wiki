## Reading the Room: How New Faces Interact With Anomaly Types

The trickiest part of any home alone anomalies new characters update is that new characters do not create a separate category of anomaly. They redistribute the existing ones. Two players can stare at the same hallway and disagree, because one is checking the person and the other is checking the environment.

Here is a working taxonomy that holds up across most builds in this genre:

| Anomaly family | Typical tell | Does a new roster affect it? |
|---|---|---|
| Presence anomalies | Someone or something that should not be there | Yes — heavily |
| Absence anomalies | A character who should be present is missing | Yes — heavily |
| Position anomalies | A character or object moved slightly | Yes — new walking routes |
| State anomalies | Posture, expression, or lighting altered | Sometimes |
| Object anomalies | Furniture, doors, or props changed | Rarely |
| Audio anomalies | Wrong sound, wrong direction, wrong timing | Sometimes |

The practical takeaway: when a roster expands, spend your first few runs re-learning presence and absence. Those two families absorb most of the change, and they are also the ones players skip because they feel too obvious to bother with.

## A Verification Routine That Survives Any Roster Change

Good players do not memorize answers. They memorize a sequence. A fixed order of checks keeps you from fixating on one corner of the screen while an anomaly strolls past behind you. A roster change like the home alone anomalies new characters update is the ideal moment to rebuild that sequence from scratch.

### Step-by-Step Sweep

| Step | Action | Time to spend |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Freeze at the doorway and count figures | 2–3 seconds |
| 2 | Compare the count to your baseline for that room | Instant |
| 3 | Sweep left to right for posture and expression changes | 3–4 seconds |
| 4 | Check the two or three objects you treat as anchors | 2 seconds |
| 5 | Listen for one beat before moving on | 2 seconds |
| 6 | Commit to a decision and do not re-scan | — |

That final step is the one people ignore. Re-scanning after you have already made a call is how doubt turns into a wrong answer. If you followed the sequence, trust it.

### Build Your Own Baseline Chart

Because the roster changed, any baseline you built before the update is stale. Rebuild it deliberately:

1. Walk the house without judging anything. Just observe who is where.
2. Write down the expected occupant of each room in a single line.
3. Run the house two or three more times and note what stays constant.
4. Only then start flagging anomalies.

This sounds slow, and it is — for about four runs. After that, accuracy jumps sharply, because you are comparing against a real baseline instead of a half-remembered one.

## Common Mistakes That Cost Runs

Most failed runs in home alone anomalies new characters patches come down to four habits:

- **Trusting old memory.** Pre-update baselines are not just outdated, they are actively misleading.
- **Fixating on one anomaly type.** If you only check furniture, you will miss everything attached to people.
- **Over-checking.** Standing in a room for thirty seconds invites false positives.
- **Ignoring audio.** New characters often arrive with new sound signatures, and sound is the cheapest early warning you have.

| Mistake | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stale baseline | Calling normal scenes anomalous | Rebuild the baseline after every roster change |
| Single-channel scanning | Missing character-based anomalies | Alternate between people and objects |
| Analysis paralysis | Standing still, second-guessing | Cap each room at roughly ten seconds |
| Playing muted | Missing audio tells entirely | Use headphones at low volume |

One more habit worth building: after a wrong call, write down what you actually saw. Patterns emerge fast. Players who keep even a rough log tend to tighten their accuracy within a handful of sessions, and they stop blaming the update for what is really a baseline problem.
