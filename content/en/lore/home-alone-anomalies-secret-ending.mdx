## Who the visitors are, and why the game withholds answers

The central tension in Home Alone: Anomalies is trust. Visitors arrive at the door and make claims. Some claims are true and some are not, and the official page frames the player's job as deciding who to believe. It does not publish a roster of visitors, a catalogue of anomaly types, or a flowchart of outcomes.

That omission is deliberate as far as anyone outside the studio can tell, and it matters for how you read community content. Third-party guides describe patterns they have observed — repeated dialogue lines, behavior that seems inconsistent with a visitor's stated identity, cues that a porch light choice was right or wrong. Those observations can be useful, but they are player reporting, not developer documentation. They can also be outdated the moment an update ships, which is a real risk in a game that received its twelfth update in roughly five months.

A practical approach: treat every guide, including careful ones, as a hypothesis. Test it in a private or small server where a wrong guess costs you less. If a guide's advice stops working, the official page is the place to check whether an update changed something.

The biggest mistake is confusing this game with similarly named experiences. If a guide describes mechanics like scanning a room for shifted objects and reporting them, you are reading about a different game.

If a site lists codes for this experience, that is a strong signal the site is generating content rather than reporting it. Redeemable codes are announced on official channels when they exist; absent that, assume there is nothing to redeem.
