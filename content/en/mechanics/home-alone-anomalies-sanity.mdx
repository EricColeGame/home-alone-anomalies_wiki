The premise of [Home Alone: Anomalies](https://www.roblox.com/games/87468080405188/Home-Alone-Anomalies) is simple to say and hard to do well: you are home alone, people keep showing up at the door, and not all of them are telling the truth. The official Roblox page describes the loop as checking cameras, listening to what visitors say, opening the door if they need help, and turning off the porch light if something is off. Survive the night and you earn cash; the game also ships troll items meant to be used on friends.

That is the whole frame. What follows separates what the developer page actually confirms from what third-party guides report, and says plainly where the public record goes quiet. If you want to check any of it yourself, the live listing is the authority.

## The core mechanic is a trust decision, not a reaction test

Most anomaly-spotting games on Roblox ask you to notice a visual difference and report it. Home Alone: Anomalies is built around a two-part judgment instead: gather information, then commit to an action that has consequences.

The official description gives you three inputs — cameras, visitor dialogue, and the state of the porch light — and two levers — open the door, or kill the light. That structure matters because it means the game is testing whether you can hold two ideas at once: what a visitor claims, and what your other evidence supports. A person who says they need help is not automatically a threat. A person who looks normal is not automatically safe. The porch light is the escape hatch, and the official page frames it as the response for when something is off, not when something is obviously wrong.

Practically, that suggests a habit rather than a trick: form your read before you touch anything. Check the camera feed, listen to the full line of dialogue rather than the first few words, then decide. Players who act on the first signal tend to be the ones who get burned, which is exactly the tension the design is going for.

## What third-party guides add, and where they stop

Community write-ups outside Roblox describe the same basic loop the developer page does and go further into play tactics — typically emphasizing that visitors should not be trusted at face value and that the porch light functions as your primary defensive tool. Treat these as secondary observation, not developer documentation. They are useful for shaping your instincts, but they are not patch notes and they are not authoritative about what any specific night will contain.

Some of these guides drift into listing visitor archetypes or anomaly names. Be careful there. The official page does not publish a roster of visitors, an anomaly taxonomy, a house layout, a list of endings, or any code strings. Any specific claim of that kind you encounter is either datamined, community-inferred, or invented, and the game can change it in a patch without notice. The October 10 update alone shows how quickly the content surface moves.

## Troll items and the social layer

The official page explicitly advertises troll items for friends, which tells you something about intended play: this is a game designed to be funnier with other people than alone. With up to 18 players per server, the social variables are real. A friend using a troll item on you changes your night in ways no single-player guide can predict.

That also means trusting the wrong visitor might not be the only way to lose. The description does not detail how troll items function, whether they can be blocked, or whether they interact with the trust decision at all. Verify in-game rather than assuming.
