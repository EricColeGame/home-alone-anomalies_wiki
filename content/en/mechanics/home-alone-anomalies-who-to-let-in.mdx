## What Are Home Alone Anomalies?

The doorbell rings. You are alone, the porch light is buzzing, and the person outside looks almost like your neighbor — almost. In home alone anomalies, who to let in is the only decision that matters, and one wrong call can end a run that took twenty minutes to build.

The premise behind home alone anomalies is simple: you are home alone, visitors arrive, and some of them are subtly wrong. Your job is to compare what you see right now against what you remember from the start of the night, then decide whether the person on the other side of the door is real or an anomaly.

That memory comparison is the whole game. Nothing else in the experience matters as much as your baseline.

### Why the Baseline Is Everything

Before the first knock, most versions of this experience give you a clean, quiet house. Nothing is broken. The mail is where it should be. The porch looks normal. This is your baseline, and it is the single most valuable thing you own.

Players who skip the baseline phase — treating it as filler before the "real" game starts — are the same players who let an anomaly in during the first few minutes. Community reports consistently point to the same failure: people remember the *idea* of their porch, not the details of it.

So spend your quiet time well. Note the color of the door, the position of the mat, the number of windows visible from the front, the way the porch light hums. You will need all of it.

## The Knock-Check-Decide Loop

Every visitor pushes you through the same three-phase loop. Understanding the loop matters more than memorizing any single anomaly, because the loop is what keeps you alive when the anomalies get subtle.

| Phase | What You Do | What to Watch For | Cost of Rushing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Knock** | Stop moving and listen before you approach | Rhythm, force, number of knocks, whether a voice follows | You miss audio anomalies entirely |
| **Check** | Observe the visitor through the peephole or a window from a fixed distance | Face, clothing, shadow, posture, surroundings | You burn your only clean look on a half-glance |
| **Decide** | Commit to letting them in or turning them away | Whether your evidence is one mismatch or two | You flip-flop and lose the window |

The key insight, repeated across player experience threads, is that **Check** deserves the most time and usually gets the least. Most players spend two seconds on the check and thirty on the decision, which is exactly backwards.

## Anomaly Types and Their Telltale Signs

Anomalies in this kind of experience fall into a handful of families. Knowing the families helps you scan faster, because you know what *kind* of wrong you are looking for instead of just staring.

| Anomaly Family | Where It Shows Up | Typical Tell | Risk If Missed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Visual** | Face, clothing, silhouette | Wrong eye color, extra finger, no shadow under the porch light | High |
| **Behavioral** | Movement and speech | Repeats a line, never blinks, wrong walking cadence | Medium-High |
| **Environmental** | Porch, yard, hallway behind them | Mat moved, mailbox flag flipped, light color shifted | Medium |
| **Audio** | Knock and voice | Off-beat knock, strange echo, uses a name you never gave | Medium |
| **Contextual** | The situation itself | Claims to be someone who should not be there tonight | High |

Visual anomalies are the ones new players catch. Contextual anomalies are the ones that end veteran runs, because nothing about the visitor *looks* wrong — the wrongness is in the story they tell.

### Compound Anomalies

Late in a run, you may face a visitor with two or more small mismatches at once. Community reports describe these as compound anomalies, and they are deliberately designed so that each individual sign is weak enough to excuse on its own. Two weak signs together are almost never a coincidence. Treat them as one strong sign.

## Who to Let In: A Practical Decision Matrix

This is the table to keep in your head. It will not cover every visitor, but it covers the situations that cause most bad decisions.

| What You Observe | Likely Category | Recommended Action | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visitor matches your baseline exactly, no other oddities | Normal | Let them in | High |
| One small mismatch, nothing else | Ambiguous | Hold and re-observe from a second angle | Low |
| Two or more mismatches | Anomaly | Do not open | High |
| Visitor knows your name, but you do not know theirs | Anomaly | Do not open | Medium-High |
| Visitor is urgent, pleading, or insistent | Pressure tactic | Do not open | High |
| Visitor refuses to step fully into the light | Anomaly | Do not open | Medium-High |
| Visitor is someone who should be home already | Contextual | Do not open | High |

Notice the pattern: urgency is never evidence. In home alone anomalies, who to let in is a question answered by observation, not by how loudly someone asks.

### The "Second Angle" Rule

When a visitor sits in the ambiguous row, do not simply stare harder from the same spot. Move. Look from a window, then from the peephole, then from a different room. Anomalies are usually built to hold up under one viewing angle, and many break when viewed from a second one. If the visitor looks identical from two separate angles, your confidence jumps sharply.

## A Step-by-Step Door Routine

Routines beat instinct. Run this sequence every single time, even when you are sure.

| Step | Action | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Freeze and listen for five full seconds | Catches audio anomalies before visuals distract you |
| 2 | Approach the door at normal speed | Rushing changes what you notice |
| 3 | Observe from a fixed distance | Moving while observing distorts your comparison |
| 4 | Compare against your baseline, item by item | Turns a vague feeling into a count |
| 5 | Cross-check one environmental detail | Cheap, fast, and catches sloppy anomalies |
| 6 | Decide, then commit without second-guessing | Half-decisions are how people open doors by accident |
| 7 | Log the outcome mentally | Builds the pattern recognition you need later |

### Common Mistakes That End Runs

| Mistake | Why It Happens | The Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Opening on reflex | Habit from real life | Count to three before touching the handle |
| Overthinking a clean visitor | Paranoia after a bad round | One mismatch means observe, two means refuse |
| Ignoring audio cues | Visual focus | Always listen before you look |
| Caving to repeated knocking | Social pressure | Knock frequency never changes the correct answer |
| Letting your baseline drift | Memory decay | Re-anchor at the start of every round |
| Trusting a familiar face instantly | Recognition bias | Familiarity is the most common disguise |

That last row deserves emphasis. In home alone anomalies, who to let in is hardest when the visitor looks like someone you trust, because your brain skips the check entirely. Force the check anyway.

## Player-Tested Tips and How Difficulty Scales

Anomaly frequency and subtlety both climb as a run progresses. Most players report the same rough arc:

| Stage | Anomaly Frequency | Subtlety | What Actually Changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early | Low | Obvious | You are learning the baseline |
| Mid | Mixed | Moderate | More visitors, shorter decision windows |
| Late | High | Very subtle | Compound anomalies and pressure tactics |

A few tips that hold up across the whole arc:

- **Trust counts over feelings.** "Something feels off" is not evidence. "Two mismatches" is.
- **Never open a door you have not looked through.** Even a fast look beats no look.
- **Re-anchor your baseline after every visitor.** The house changes subtly as the night goes on.
- **Watch the environment, not just the person.** Sloppy anomalies leave traces behind them.
- **Accept that you will lose runs.** The genre is built on repetition and pattern learning.

## FAQ

**How do I know who to let in when the visitor looks completely normal?**
Check the context, not the face. Does this person have a reason to be here right now? Did they use a name you never gave? Perfect appearance plus a broken story is still an anomaly. When it comes to home alone anomalies, who to let in should be decided by evidence you can name out loud.

**Is it ever correct to let an anomaly inside?**
In most versions, no. Letting an anomaly in usually ends the run or triggers a fail state. If you are unsure, refusing is the safer error — a missed normal visitor costs far less than an admitted anomaly.

**What is the most common anomaly players miss?**
Audio and contextual anomalies. Players train themselves to stare at faces, so a wrong knock rhythm or a visitor who claims to be someone who should not be there slips past. Listen first, then look.

**Do repeated knocks mean I should open the door?**
No. Insistence is a pressure tactic, not new information. The correct answer does not change because someone knocked a fourth time. Hold your decision and keep observing.
