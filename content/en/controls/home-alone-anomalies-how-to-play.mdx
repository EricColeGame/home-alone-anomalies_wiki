## Home Alone Anomalies Controls: Default Keybinds and What Each One Does

The control scheme is intentionally minimal. You are not fighting anything — you are inspecting, comparing, and deciding. That means almost every input maps to movement, looking, or interacting.

Most players find the default layout comfortable, though bindings can differ slightly between builds and platforms, so confirm yours in the in-game settings menu before your first night.

| Action | Keyboard default | Gamepad default | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Move | W / A / S / D | Left stick | Slow, deliberate walking reveals more than sprinting |
| Look / free camera | Mouse | Right stick | Needed for scanning ceilings, corners, and doorframes |
| Interact / inspect | E | A / Cross | Opens drawers, doors, notes, and triggers reports |
| Sprint | Shift | Left stick click | Use sparingly — noise and speed both cost you |
| Crouch | Ctrl | B / Circle | Lowers your profile and slows your breathing audio |
| Flashlight toggle | F | D-pad up | Light reveals detail but also announces your position |
| Journal / anomaly log | Tab or J | D-pad left | Your memory aid — the single most underrated tool |
| Pause / settings | Esc | Start | Also where you remap every binding above |
| Voice chat (co-op) | V | — | Essential when splitting up with a partner |

If you only remap one thing, put the journal on a key your left hand can hit without leaving movement. Players who constantly check their log catch far more inconsistencies than players who try to hold everything in their head.

## The Nightly Gameplay Loop, Step by Step

Every session follows roughly the same rhythm. Understanding the phases matters because the game's tension comes from knowing that a decision is coming — not from a jump scare.

| Phase | What happens | What you should be doing |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Arrival | You start in a familiar room with the house in a "baseline" state | Walk a quick lap and memorize the room layout |
| 2. Exploration | Doors unlock, lights behave, objects settle into place | Note anything that feels off but do not report yet |
| 3. Anomaly window | One or more changes are now active somewhere in the house | Compare against your baseline, not against your memory of last run |
| 4. Reporting | You confirm whether an anomaly exists and what it is | Commit only when you are confident |
| 5. Escalation | The house reacts — lights, sounds, and layout shift | Stay calm, keep moving, and do not panic-report |

The critical insight is phase 3. New players treat every oddity as an anomaly. Veterans treat oddities as candidates and only escalate the ones they can describe precisely. Precision is the whole skill ceiling.

A practical habit: after each successful night, spend thirty seconds writing down two or three details you are certain about — the color of a lamp, the number of chairs, which door was open. That short list becomes your baseline for the next run.

## How to Spot Anomalies Before They Spot You

Anomalies generally fall into a handful of families. Once you know the families, your eyes know where to go.

| Anomaly family | What to look for | Common tell |
|---|---|---|
| Object displacement | Furniture, dishes, or decor moved a few inches | Shadows land differently than the object suggests |
| Duplication | An extra chair, an extra portrait, a second clock | Two identical items where one should exist |
| Removal | A missing painting, an absent rug, a vanished pet bowl | Empty hooks, clean patches on dusty floors |
| Lighting and color | Warmer or cooler tones, a bulb that should be off | Contrast between adjacent rooms |
| Sound cues | Breathing, ticking, footsteps that do not match your own | Audio that continues when you stand still |
| Text and signage | Notes, labels, or posters that read differently | Spelling or phrasing that feels "off" |
| Physics oddities | Objects floating, doors swinging the wrong way | Items that ignore gravity or momentum |
| Presence | A silhouette, a figure at a window, a shape in a doorway | Movement in your peripheral vision |

### Use a fixed scanning route

Do not wander randomly. Pick a route — say, front door, hallway, kitchen, stairs, bedrooms, back to the front door — and walk it the same way every night. A repeatable route turns anomaly detection into a spot-the-difference puzzle instead of a memory test. Community reports consistently suggest that route discipline is the biggest single factor separating players who clear nights consistently from players who flame out.

### Trust your audio

Sound design does a lot of heavy lifting here. If a room sounds wrong before it looks wrong, believe the sound. Standing still for five seconds in each room is a legitimate strategy, not a waste of time.

### Log before you leap

Use the journal for anything you are more than 60% sure about. If you are below that threshold, keep looking. Reporting a false anomaly usually costs more than missing one.

## Settings, Difficulty, and Co-op Setup

Before your first run, spend five minutes in the options menu. It changes the experience more than most players expect.

| Setting | What it typically changes | Recommended for a first run |
|---|---|---|
| Difficulty | Frequency and subtlety of anomalies | Start low to learn the baseline loop |
| Flashlight behavior | Battery drain or unlimited light | Unlimited, until you know the layout |
| Subtitles / captions | On-screen audio cues | On — sound cues are core information |
| Brightness | Visibility in dark rooms | Raise slightly above default |
| Mouse sensitivity | Camera speed | Lower than your usual FPS setting |
| Voice chat | Push-to-talk vs. open mic | Push-to-talk for co-op |

### Co-op tips

If you are playing with a partner, split the house into zones and agree on who owns which. Two players covering the same hallway is wasted coverage. Designate one person as the reporter — the one who commits the answer — so you never have two conflicting calls in the same window. Decide in advance how you will signal "I found something" versus "I need help."

## Five Mistakes That End Runs Early

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Reporting on instinct | A wrong report often resets your progress | Require a specific, describable detail before you commit |
| Sprinting everywhere | You miss small changes and make noise | Walk your route; sprint only to escape |
| Skipping rooms | Anomalies hide in low-traffic areas | Clear the whole house, even boring corners |
| Relying on memory alone | Memory drifts after a few nights | Use the journal and a written baseline |
| Panicking when the house reacts | Panic causes false reports | Stop, breathe, and re-scan the nearest room |

One more habit worth building: when you get an anomaly wrong, do not restart immediately. Walk the house once more and identify what fooled you. Most players repeat the same class of mistake — usually lighting or sound — until they consciously correct it.

## FAQ

**How do I learn the controls quickly?**
Play one full night without trying to win. Just walk the route, open everything, and press every interaction key you can find. Familiarity with the Home Alone Anomalies how to play basics comes from repetition, not from reading a list.

**Do I need to report every anomaly I see?**
No. Report only what you can describe precisely. Over-reporting is a more common failure state than under-reporting among new players.

**Is co-op easier than solo?**
It is different rather than strictly easier. Two sets of eyes cover more ground, but coordination adds a new failure point. Agree on zones and on who makes the final call.

**What is the best way to practice spotting anomalies?**
Use a fixed walking route, keep the journal open, and review your mistakes after each failed night. Player experience suggests that roughly a handful of focused sessions is enough to internalize a house's baseline.

**Can I change the difficulty mid-run?**
Difficulty is usually set at the start of a session, so pick your level before you begin. Check the in-game options menu for the exact behavior in your build, since it can vary between updates.
