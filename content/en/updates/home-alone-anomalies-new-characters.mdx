Most horror games about being home alone hand you a flashlight and a hiding spot. [Home Alone: Anomalies](https://www.roblox.com/games/87468080405188/Home-Alone-Anomalies) hands you a decision instead: someone is knocking, and you have to work out whether opening the door is an act of kindness or the last thing you ever do.

That premise — a night alone, a stream of visitors, and a nagging sense that not all of them are telling the truth — is what makes the experience stand out in a crowded genre of Roblox anomaly-spotting games. This article sticks to what the official Roblox page documents, separates that from what players report, and is honest about the parts nobody outside the game can confirm.

## What Home Alone: Anomalies Actually Is

According to the official Roblox record, Home Alone: Anomalies is a Roblox experience created by the groups 3 AM Productions and Freeground. The place was created on May 4, 2026, last updated on October 10, 2026, supports up to 18 players per server, and has accumulated roughly 208 million visits.

The official description frames the loop plainly: you are home alone, visitors keep arriving at the door, and not everyone is who they claim to be. Your job is to decide who to trust and survive the night.

Before going further, one clarification matters. This is **not** *Anomaly Watch*. It is also unrelated to anomaly-hunting games where you scan a room for changes. The word "anomalies" here refers to something being off about a visitor or the situation outside your house, not to a hidden-object checklist.

## The Core Loop: Door, Voice, Camera, Light

The official page lists four advertised actions, and they form the spine of every night:

| Advertised action | What it asks you to judge |
| --- | --- |
| Check the cameras | What is actually outside, beyond what you're told |
| Listen to what visitors say | Whether their story holds together |
| Open the door if they need help | Whether the request is genuine |
| Turn off the porch light if something is off | Whether to signal "go away" instead of "come in" |

Read that as a system of cross-checking rather than a checklist of chores. A visitor's words are one data point. The camera feed is another. The porch light is your only visible response short of the door itself — and it appears to function as a rejection, which is why the description pairs it specifically with the phrase "if something is off."

Because the game supports up to 18 players, the reasoning is not necessarily a solo exercise. The official page does not explain how roles are split or whether all players share the same view of the door. That is something you can verify on your first server by watching who reacts and when.

## What the Halloween Update Added

The official description includes Update 12, dated October 10, 2026 — a Halloween event. The listed content is specific and worth quoting accurately rather than embellishing:

- Collectible pumpkins and a Halloween Pass
- Costumed visitors
- New pets: Black Cat, White Cat, and Crawler Cat
- A Top 100 pumpkin leaderboard, with a Celestial Cat as the reward
- Bug fixes and additional changes

A few reasonable inferences follow, flagged as inferences. Costumed visitors imply the Halloween event changes how you evaluate the person at the door — a costume is, after all, the oldest trick in the book, and the game's whole premise is that appearances lie. Collectible pumpkins imply some form of in-game scavenging layered on top of the survival night, and a Top 100 leaderboard implies a finite, ranked reward rather than a participation prize.

What the official page does **not** say: pumpkin spawn locations, event end date, pass pricing, pet abilities, or whether Black Cat, White Cat and Crawler Cat behave differently from one another. Don't trust anyone who fills those gaps with confident numbers.

## How to Reason About a Visitor

The developer description does not publish a list of visitor types, anomaly names, or a decision flowchart, so any "complete list" you see circulating is a third-party construction rather than documentation. Community guides that describe checking the camera before answering, comparing what you see against what you hear, and erring toward the porch light when details don't match are consistent with the official feature list — but they are secondary observations, not developer-confirmed rules. Treat them as a starting hypothesis, not gospel.

A defensible approach that requires no outside knowledge:

1. **Let them talk first.** The description explicitly tells you to listen. A visitor's stated reason for needing help is the claim you're testing.
2. **Verify with the camera.** If the camera can't corroborate the claim, you have a conflict between two sources.
3. **Prefer the low-commitment response.** The porch light is reversible in the sense that it doesn't let anyone in. The door is not.
4. **Accept that you'll sometimes be wrong.** A survival game built on trust decisions only works if some honest visitors look suspicious. Following any guide guarantees nothing.

## Earning Cash, Troll Items, and Other Advertised Extras

Two more advertised features are easy to overlook. First, surviving the night earns cash — the official page states this directly, though it does not publish amounts or a currency breakdown. Second, the page mentions troll items for friends, which suggests the experience is designed to be played in company rather than strictly alone, despite the title. Again, names, prices and effects are not documented on the official page; check the in-game shop if you want specifics.

## Why the Ambiguity Is the Point

The strongest argument for Home Alone: Anomalies is that its advertised mechanics are all information-gathering tools with genuinely unclear outputs. A camera, a voice and a light switch shouldn't be a tense toolkit, but they are, because the game withholds the rulebook on purpose. That's also why third-party write-ups fill the vacuum so eagerly — and why you should read them skeptically and confirm anything important by playing.
